Abstract. AI findings reach most people as retellings, and the hype debate runs on anecdote. We build a claim-level instrument. For eight papers (six on model misbehaviour, two deflationary) we extract the claims authors make in their abstract and limitations text, have a four-model panel label each a central result or a scope limit, and score which claims retellings keep. Because short texts keep fewer claims, the estimand is within-document: D = share of scope limits stated minus share of results stated. The analysis was pre-registered. In 87 news articles sampled by a fixed rule, D̄ = -0.26 [-0.33, -0.20]: articles keep results more than scope limits; a second scorer agrees and the sign survives dropping any paper. Lab blog posts show the same tilt and headlines sharpen it. Closed-book Claude answers also gave D̄ = -0.23 [-0.30, -0.16], the opposite of what our pilot predicted. When models answered a question from a supplied article, scope limits present in the article survived less often than results (d = -0.16 [-0.27, -0.06]). A generic instruction to keep caveats removed the gap (d = +0.01 [-0.08, +0.10]), a sham card did not, and a six-claim card drawn from the authors own text removed it too and raised card claims in the answer from 29% to 64%, including claims the article omitted. The scorer agrees with blind human labels (kappa 0.86) but failed one pre-registered calibration test, so by our own rule these comparisons are exploratory. We release cards, scores and a checker.

1. Introduction

A model that blackmails a fictional engineer, fakes alignment or sabotages its shutdown is now a staple of public argument about AI. So is the study showing AI tools slowed developers down. Few people read these papers. They read an article or a headline, or ask a chatbot. If a retelling keeps the result and drops the conditions under which it held, readers over-generalise; if it keeps the conditions and drops the result, they conclude nothing happened. Both are failures of the information chain that safety decisions rely on, and the debate about which failure dominates is run on anecdotes.

The question is measurable because authors state both sides themselves: results in the abstract, scope limits in the abstract and limitations section. A retelling can be checked against those statements without anyone ruling on whether the research is right. This is the kind of cheap, auditable, machine-readable signal the trust-infrastructure agenda asks for, and it comes with a testable use: does giving an AI system the authors' own claims change what it tells a user?

Our main contributions are:

  1. A claim-level, two-sided fidelity instrument whose content is not chosen by the investigator: claims are extracted by two models, roles assigned by a four-model panel, the display card drawn at random, every quote string-matched to the paper.
  2. A length-robust estimand and a pre-registered analysis (commit a8f106d, four amendments logged, three before scoring) with three confirmatory tests and a validity gate the instrument can fail.
  3. Measurements on a rule-sampled news corpus, its headlines and closed-book chatbot answers for eight papers of both valences.
  4. An experiment on whether models answering from a news article keep scope limits and results at different rates, and whether a generic instruction, a sham card or a real card changes that.

2. Related Work

Information change from paper to news. Sumner et al. (2014, 2016) traced exaggeration and caveats in health news to university press releases; Yavchitz et al. (2012) did the same for "spin" in trial reports. Wright et al. (2022) model how much information a news or social-media sentence preserves from a paper finding, and Wührl et al. (2024) annotate fine-grained distortions (causality, certainty, generality, sensationalism) in paper-to-news and paper-to-tweet pairs. We differ in unit and target: a fixed pool of the authors' own claims per paper, split by role, for AI research, with chatbots as a retelling channel.

LLMs retelling science. Peters and Chin-Yee (2025) show LLM summaries of scientific papers over-generalise relative to the originals. Their metric is one-sided (generalisation); ours asks which side is dropped.

Method lineage. Scoring a text for the presence of pre-specified content units is the Pyramid method (Nenkova and Passonneau, 2004) and nugget evaluation, now automated with LLMs (Pradeep et al., 2024); atomic-fact scoring follows FActScore (Min et al., 2023). Our instrument is a special case of nugget recall. What we add is where the nuggets come from (authors' abstract and limitations), the role split, the within-document contrast, and the intervention test.

AI in the media. Kapoor and Narayanan (2022) catalogue recurring pitfalls in AI journalism qualitatively. Summerfield et al. (2025) criticise scheming research itself; we take no position on that and treat the pool as "what the authors assert".

3. Methods

3.1 Claim pools and cards

For each paper we took the arXiv abstract and the limitations (or caveats) section. Two extractors from different families (Claude Sonnet, Qwen 3.5 9B) independently split this text into atomic claims with a verbatim quote; a merge step kept the union. Four raters (Claude Haiku, Claude Sonnet, Qwen 3.5 9B, Gemma 3 4B) then labelled each claim, blind, as result (what was observed, or evidence it is robust), scope (narrows what the result applies to, how strong it is, or how far it generalises) or background. Claims without a majority and background claims were dropped. Of 189 rated claims, 117 remain. Krippendorff's alpha for role was 0.56: the result/scope distinction is only moderately reliable, which bounds everything downstream. A card is a seeded random draw of three result and three scope claims (Appendix A); there is no importance ranking, so the card and the remaining pool are exchangeable.

3.2 Estimand

For a document, D = (share of the paper's scope claims it states) − (share of result claims it states), over the full pool. D > 0 means scope limits survive better than results. A document's length limits both shares alike, so D compares what a retelling chose to keep within its own space. Per-finding Df is the document mean; D̄ weights findings equally. Findings are fixed strata, not a sample: we make no claim about AI research in general.

3.3 Corpus

News. Eight fixed queries per finding to Google News RSS, restricted to 90 days from publication; every returned item was resolved, fetched and kept unless it failed a pre-set exclusion (lab-owned, social, aggregator or wire domain; body under 150 words; paywall stub; near-duplicate; scorer-judged not about the paper). 219 items were retrieved, 138 kept after fetching and exclusions, and 87 were relevant and inside the primary 0 to 30 day window for the six full-pipeline findings (Table 1). This is "what Google News surfaces for these queries in 2026", not a census of coverage. Headlines are the RSS titles of the same articles. Chatbots: three fixed lay phrasings per finding, no web access; Claude Haiku and Sonnet (two samples per phrasing, run as tool-less helpers inside a coding harness). An answer qualifies only if the model was released at least 90 days after the paper and the answer is about the paper.

3.4 Scoring and calibration

A scorer reads one retelling with the paper's whole pool and labels each claim stated, absent or contradicted, quoting evidence (rubric in the repository). Modal GPU access failed on the day, so the planned full second scorer from another model family was not possible. The primary scorer is Claude Haiku on everything. Claude Sonnet independently scored a seeded subset of 44 news articles for the T1 check, and local Qwen 3.5 9B scored part of the calibration set (amendments 2 and 4; amendment 4 was made after the primary news result was seen).

Perturbed real documents. From real articles and answers we made minimal edits with known effect: delete the sentence carrying a stated claim, insert a rephrased claim sentence, or insert a sentence asserting the opposite. For the primary scorer, insertion was detected 100% [91, 100], n=39; deletion 58% [42, 72], n=38; contradiction 92% [80, 97], n=39; labels of untouched claims were unchanged 93% [91, 94], n=1310. On easier spec-written synthetic retellings sensitivity was 100% [95, 100], n=74 and specificity 89% [84, 93], n=160.

Human labels. The author labelled 120 judgements on 20 randomly drawn documents, blind to hop and scorer output: agreement with the primary scorer 93%, Cohen kappa 0.86 [0.76, 0.94]. This is one annotator who is also the author. Gate V0 failed: it required the lower confidence bound of both insertion and deletion accuracy to reach 85%, and deletion did not. Under our own rule every comparison below is therefore labelled exploratory. A post hoc look explains the failure without excusing it: in every deletion miss the scorer quoted a different sentence, still present in the edited text, that also conveys the claim, so removing one sentence did not remove the claim. Deletion as we built it is not a clean specificity test; agreement with human labels and the synthetic set suggest specificity near 90%, but that is not the test we pre-registered.

Across 44 documents scored by both scorers, agreement on stated versus not was 92% (kappa 0.82).

4. Results

T1: news. D̄ = -0.26 [-0.33, -0.20] over 87 articles (98.3% interval, documents resampled within finding). Pre-registered decision: D != 0. On the subset scored by the secondary scorer D̄ = -0.33 [-0.41, -0.24]. Leave-one-finding-out estimates range from -0.32 to -0.19. Sensitivity windows: 0 to 14 days -0.27 [-0.34, -0.21]; 31 to 90 days -0.20 [-0.28, -0.12]. Corrected for measured misclassification, D̄ = -0.24. 5. Discussion and Limitations

What this shows. On pools of the authors' own claims, every retelling layer we measured keeps central results more readily than scope limits: lab blog posts, news articles, headlines, chatbot answers from memory, and model answers written from a supplied article. The size is similar across layers (D between about −0.2 and −0.3 where estimated), it appears for deflationary papers as well as alarming ones, and it is present before journalists are involved. So the loss of scope is better described as a property of summarising than as a failing of one profession. Two cautions apply. Scope claims come largely from limitations sections and may be finer-grained than abstract claims, so some of the gap is a property of how papers are written. And D says which claims are omitted, not that the omission misled anyone.

The experiment is the part with a control. When a model answers from an article, scope limits that are in the article are dropped more often than results. A one-line instruction to keep key caveats removed that imbalance, which is a cheap fix worth knowing about; unrelated true facts in card format did nothing, so the effect is not from attaching a list. The card did what the instruction cannot: it brought in author claims the article never contained, doubling the share of card claims in the answer, without crowding out other content. For retrieval systems the implication is concrete. An instruction can rebalance what a source contains; only source-attributed content from the authors can restore what the source omitted.

Use. The cards are plain JSON with verbatim quotes. A writer can check a draft, a reader a clip, and a summarising agent can fetch the card with the article it is reading. We propose that papers likely to be widely retold ship with such a card, written or approved by the authors.

Limitations

  • Model-heavy pipeline. Extraction, role labels, the primary scorer and the chatbots under test are mostly Claude models; two of the eight papers are Anthropic's. The planned cross-family scorer ran only on subsets. Shared blind spots cannot be ruled out.
  • Role reliability. Alpha 0.56 for result versus scope means some claims sit on the wrong side; D inherits that noise.
  • Ground truth is the authors' framing. Several of these papers are contested. An omitted claim is an omission of what the authors assert, not an error of fact.
  • Sampling. Google News ranking in 2026 for 2024–25 stories, English only, 87 articles, 4 to 26 per paper; fetch failures removed about a third of items, likely more from paywalled outlets.
  • Calibration. The pre-registered gate failed on the deletion test, so all comparisons are exploratory by our own rule. Human labels come from one annotator who is the author and knew the hypotheses.
  • Chatbots. Claude only, inside a coding harness, no search; GPT, Gemini and others were not tested. T3 says nothing about consumer assistants in general.
  • Experiment. 23 articles, two readers, one question phrasing; card-claim gains under the real card are partly mechanical, which is why the sham and held-out comparisons matter.
  • Deviations. Three amendments were logged before scoring (more news queries; Modal unavailable; random rather than stratified experiment articles) and a fourth after the primary news result was seen (Sonnet, not a cross-family model, as second scorer for T1). Video and the multi-vendor appendix were cut.

Future Work

A second scorer family on the full corpus; human double-coding; author-approved cards; GPT, Gemini and search-enabled assistants; short video; and a field test of whether journalists or agents use cards when offered.

6. Conclusion

We turned an argument about hype into a measurement with a rule for being wrong: a claim pool taken from authors' own text, a length-robust contrast between scope limits and results, pre-registered tests and a validity gate. By those rules, retellings of these eight papers keep results over scope limits at every layer, a prediction from our own pilot failed, our scorer failed one of our own calibration tests, and a card of the authors' claims restored content in model answers that the source article had dropped. The cards, scores and checker are released so that others can re-score with other models, add papers, and test whether attaching authors' own claims to retellings improves what AI systems pass on.

Appendix B. Limitations and Dual-Use / Ethical Considerations

Dual use. A card can be gamed: a retelling may state all six claims and still mislead through emphasis, and cards could be written to favour a side. Restricting pools to verbatim author text, drawing cards at random and publishing every evidence quote keeps this auditable. Scores identify public articles by URL; they are automated judgements against one pool and are not ratings of an outlet. Omission is not falsehood, and short formats cannot state everything; this is why the estimand is a within-document contrast and not a count.

Collection. YouTube rate-limited transcript requests in the pilot; we stopped rather than work around the limit and cut video. Article text is not redistributed.

Conflict of perspective. Most models in the pipeline are Claude models and two studied papers are Anthropic's; results about Claude's answers need replication with other scorers and vendors.
